# Oil Refinery

An oil refinery, or petroleum refinery, is an industrial process plant where crude oil is processed and refined into more useful petroleum products, such as gasoline, diesel fuel, asphalt base, heating oil, kerosene, and liquefied petroleum gas.

## Basic Process: Distillation

The first step in refining crude oil is atmospheric distillation, carried out in a distillation column (also called a fractionating column or fractionation tower). Crude oil is heated in a furnace to around 350-400 degrees Celsius and fed into the column, where it separates into different fractions based on differing boiling points. Lighter fractions such as gases and naphtha rise to the top of the column, while heavier fractions such as residual fuel oil and bitumen are drawn off near the bottom.

## Reflux Ratio

In a distillation column, the reflux ratio is the ratio of the amount of liquid returned to the column (reflux) to the amount of liquid drawn off as distillate product. A higher reflux ratio generally improves the purity of separation between fractions but increases energy consumption, since more liquid must be re-vaporized. Operators must balance reflux ratio against energy costs and desired product purity when optimizing column setpoints.

## Column Pressure

Distillation columns can operate at atmospheric pressure, under vacuum, or under elevated pressure, depending on the fraction being separated. Vacuum distillation is often used downstream of atmospheric distillation to further separate heavy residues without needing extremely high temperatures that would cause thermal cracking of the hydrocarbons. Column pressure affects the boiling points of the components being separated and is a key controllable setpoint in refinery process optimization.

## Secondary Conversion Processes

Beyond basic distillation, refineries employ conversion processes to increase the yield of higher-value products from heavier fractions. These include catalytic cracking (which breaks large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller ones using a catalyst), hydrocracking (cracking in the presence of hydrogen), and catalytic reforming (which rearranges hydrocarbon molecules to increase octane rating for gasoline).

## Energy Consumption and Efficiency

Refineries are highly energy-intensive facilities. Furnace temperature, reflux ratio, and column pressure are among the key controllable process setpoints that operators adjust to balance throughput, product yield, energy consumption, and overall efficiency. Because refinery processes are interconnected, changing one setpoint (for example, raising furnace temperature to increase throughput) often creates trade-offs elsewhere in the system, such as increased energy consumption or reduced product quality, which is why refineries increasingly use optimization models and machine learning approaches to find setpoint combinations that balance these competing objectives.

## Safety and Emissions

Modern refineries are subject to strict environmental regulations governing emissions of sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and volatile organic compounds. Many refineries have implemented sulfur recovery units to capture and process hydrogen sulfide removed from crude oil during refining, converting it into elemental sulfur rather than releasing it into the atmosphere.
